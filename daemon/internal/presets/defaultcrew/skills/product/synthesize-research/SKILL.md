# Synthesize Research

Convert a pile of qualitative input into a short list of strong findings. The deliverable's job is to inform a decision, not to summarize everything that was said.

## Process

1. **Familiarize.** Read everything end to end before coding anything. Get a feel for the landscape; first impressions reveal what stands out.
2. **Code observations.** Tag each observation, quote, or data point with a short descriptive code. Be generous — easier to merge later than to split.
3. **Cluster into themes.** Group related codes. Let categories emerge from the data, don't force them. Each theme captures something important about the research question.
4. **Test themes against the data.** Does each have sufficient evidence? Are they distinct? Together, do they tell a coherent story?
5. **Name and rank.** Describe each theme in one or two sentences. Order by priority (frequency × impact).

## What to extract from each source

- **Observations.** What did the user describe doing, experiencing, feeling? Note context — when, where, with whom, how often.
- **Direct quotes.** Verbatim, attributed by participant type (not name): "Enterprise admin, 200-person team."
- **Behaviors vs. stated preferences.** What they DO often differs from what they SAY they want. Behavioral evidence is stronger.
- **Pain points.** Frustrations, workarounds — workarounds are unmet needs in disguise.
- **Signals of intensity.** Emotional language, frequency of encounter, effort expended on workarounds, consequence when things go wrong.

## Priority matrix

For each theme:

| | High impact | Low impact |
|---|---|---|
| **High frequency** | Top priority | Quality-of-life improvements |
| **Low frequency** | Important for specific segments | Note and deprioritize |

## Confidence calibration

Label each finding with a confidence level:

- **High.** Multiple sources, multiple methods, consistent across participants.
- **Medium.** Suggestive across a few sources; some contradiction or limited sample.
- **Low.** Early signal; one or two sources; needs more evidence.

A finding from two interviews is a hypothesis, not a conclusion. Name it as such.

## When sources disagree

Contradictions are signal, not error. They often reveal distinct user segments. Don't pick a side and bury the other — report the disagreement, name the segments, suggest what would resolve it.

## Output

- **Overview.** Methodology, sources, sample size, research question(s), timeframe.
- **Key findings** (5–8). Each: one-sentence finding, supporting quotes with attribution, frequency, impact, confidence level.
- **Segments** if the data reveals distinct ones. Behaviors, needs, pain points, representative quotes.
- **Opportunities.** Where current solutions fall short, what new capabilities would unlock value.
- **Recommendations.** Specific, actionable, tied to specific findings. "Add a progress indicator to the setup flow" — not "improve onboarding."
- **Open questions.** What the research didn't answer; what to investigate next.

## Anti-patterns

- Forcing data into a predetermined narrative.
- Treating stated preferences as equivalent to observed behavior.
- Reporting averages without distributions. A 3.5 average could mean lukewarm or polarized.
- Synthesizing too many themes. 5–8 strong findings beats 20 weak ones.
- Recommendations vague enough to fit any product ("improve user experience").
