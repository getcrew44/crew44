# Competitive Brief

Inform a decision, not a comprehensive comparison. The "so what" — strategic implications — is where the value sits. Skip it and the brief is wasted work.

## Define the competitive set

Competitors live at multiple levels. Be explicit about which you're analyzing.

- **Direct.** Same problem, same users, same approach. Appear in your deals.
- **Indirect.** Same problem, different approach (spreadsheets vs. dedicated tool).
- **Adjacent.** Don't compete today but could — adjacent platforms, startups attacking a niche that could grow.
- **Substitute.** Different way users solve the underlying need — hiring a person, manual process, doing nothing.

## Sections

1. **Competitor overview.** For each: target market, positioning claim, recent momentum (launches, funding, customer wins, key hires).
2. **Landscape map.** Position on 1–2 strategic axes that reveal differences — breadth vs. depth, self-serve vs. sales-led, SMB vs. enterprise, simple vs. powerful. Choose axes that surface real strategic dynamics.
3. **Capability comparison.** Group features by category buyers care about, not your internal architecture. Rate each competitor per area on a simple scale: **Strong / Adequate / Weak / Absent.** Rate based on real product experience and reviews, not marketing claims.
4. **Positioning analysis.** Extract each competitor's positioning statement: "For [target customer] who [need], [Product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], it [differentiator]." Pull from homepage, app store description, sales pitch language.
5. **Strengths and weaknesses.** Per competitor. Be honest — dismissing competitors makes the analysis useless. What do customers genuinely praise? What do they consistently complain about?
6. **Opportunities and threats.** Where are gaps no one fills well? Where are competitors investing heavily? Where are we most vulnerable?
7. **Strategic implications.** This is the section that earns the brief. What should we build, accelerate, or deprioritize? Where differentiate vs. achieve parity? What should we monitor going forward?

## Sources beyond marketing pages

- Customer reviews (G2, Capterra, TrustRadius) — what real users love and hate.
- Job postings — hiring signals strategic direction. ML engineers means an ML bet.
- Recent product changelogs and blog posts.
- Press, analyst coverage, earnings call language (public competitors).
- Win/loss notes from your sales team. Bias-aware but immediate.

## Capability rating — what good looks like

- Rate based on the product, not the marketing. "Has feature X" is less useful than "How well does it do X?"
- Weight by what matters to your target customers, not total feature count.
- Don't always come out ahead. A comparison that always shows you winning is not credible.
- Note "why it matters" for each capability area — buyers don't weigh features equally.

## Anti-patterns

- Dismissing competitors. Useless and inaccurate.
- Comparing on architecture instead of buyer-visible value. Customers don't care about elegance.
- Pricing comparisons without caveats (packaging differences, usage-based vs. seat-based, custom enterprise).
- Skipping strategic implications. Without "so what," the brief is content, not intelligence.
- Treating the brief as durable. Note the date; competitive analyses go stale fast.
